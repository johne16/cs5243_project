Masked Pretraining vs Random Initialization for Object Detection

How much does masked-image pretraining help object detection as labeled data becomes scarce?
Samantha Salas  ·  John Edwards
The University of Texas at San Antonio · Fall 2026

Hi, I'm Samantha Salas, and together with John Edwards we're studying how much masked-image pretraining helps object detection when labeled data is limited.

I'll cover our question, how we designed the experiment, where the project stands, and what we expect to find.

Motivation

Labels are expensive. Can unlabeled images make up for fewer of them?

The problem

  • Detectors learn from human-drawn boxes on every image.
  • Box annotation is the costly part, and accuracy falls fast when labels are few.

The idea

  • Masked image modeling: hide most of an image and train the backbone to reconstruct it, using no labels.
  • Then fine-tune the detector on the boxes we do have.
Question

How much does masked pretraining improve detection AP over random initialization, and how does the gain change as labels shrink, for a transformer and a CNN?

Hypothesis

The gap widens as labels shrink, and is larger for the transformer, which lacks a CNN's built-in inductive bias.

Object detectors normally learn only from human-drawn boxes, and drawing those boxes is the expensive part. With few labels, accuracy drops sharply.

Masked image modeling offers another signal: hide most of an image, have the backbone reconstruct it, and learn from unlabeled images before ever seeing a box.

Our question is how much that pretraining buys over random initialization, and how the benefit changes as labels shrink. We expect the gap to widen at small label fractions, and to be larger for a Vision Transformer, since it lacks the convolutional inductive bias and leans more on what pretraining provides.

Our approach

A controlled comparison, not a leaderboard

Published gains use different schedules, heads, and ImageNet pretraining, so they are hard to compare. We hold everything fixed except the starting weights.

⚙

One protocol

Both detectors run in Detectron2 with the same head, augmentation, schedule, and evaluation code.

↻

No outside data

Pretraining uses the same COCO images the detector trains on, with annotations withheld. No ImageNet.

↘

Label sweep

Train on 10% to 100% of the labels in nested splits, to see how the gain changes as labels shrink.

Prior papers report these gains under different schedules and detection heads, and they pretrain on ImageNet, so the numbers aren't directly comparable.

We control all of that. Both detectors share one Detectron2 protocol. Pretraining uses the same COCO images the detector later trains on, so no outside data enters. And we sweep the labeled fraction from ten to one hundred percent.

2 pretraining runs, then 10 label fractions × 2 arms × 2 backbones

At every label fraction we train the detector twice. In the pretrained arm, the backbone first learns from unlabeled images with MAE or SparK. In the random arm, it starts from random weights. Everything after that is identical, so the difference in AP is what pretraining bought.

We test two families: a ViT-Tiny with MAE in ViTDet, and a ResNet-18 with SparK in Faster R-CNN. We use tiny backbones to fit our compute. That gives two pretraining runs and forty detection runs.

What we will measure

The headline result: AP gain at each label fraction

Illustrative · hypothesis, not data

How we'll read it

  • Curve falls left to right: pretraining matters most when labels are scarce.
  • Flat curve: pretraining adds a constant boost, regardless of label count.
  • ViT above ResNet: transformers depend more on pretraining.

Secondary metrics: AP50, AP75, and AP by object size, to see where the gain comes from.

Our main result will be this plot: the AP gain from pretraining at each label fraction. What's shown is our hypothesis, not data.

If the curve falls from left to right, pretraining matters most when labels are scarce. A flat curve means it's a constant boost. And if the ViT curve sits above the ResNet curve, transformers rely more on pretraining. We'll also break AP down by IoU threshold and object size to see where the gain comes from.

Challenges

What could bias the result, and how we handle it

RiskWhy it mattersMitigation
Undertrained scratch armFrom-scratch training converges slower; a short schedule overstates pretraining's benefitReport AP over training so undertraining is visible
Noise at 10% labelsWhich images get sampled can swing AP as much as the effect itselfRepeat small fractions with different seeds
Porting two codebasesMAE and SparK differ in normalization, LR scaling, and layer decayOne shared Detectron2 pipeline; check settings against the originals
Limited computeTiny backbones and a reduced pool put absolute AP below published valuesCompare gaps between arms, not absolute AP

A few things could bias the comparison. The biggest is that training from scratch converges slower, so a short schedule would exaggerate pretraining's benefit; we'll report AP over the course of training to make that visible.

At ten percent of labels, sampling noise can be as large as the effect, so we repeat those runs with different seeds. Porting two codebases into Detectron2 risks silently changing one arm, so we check settings against the originals. And because of compute, our absolute AP will be low, so we focus on the gap, not the raw number.

Summary
One controlled protocol, two backbone families, ten label fractions: a direct measure of what unlabeled images are worth when boxes are scarce.
